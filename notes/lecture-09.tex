\section{Review: normal subgroups}

Last time we introduced the notion of normal subgroups.

\begin{define}
A subgroup $N\leq G$ is said to be \emph{normal} if
\[
  gNg^{-1}\subseteq N\qquad\text{for every }g\in G.
\]
As we saw last time, this is equivalent to requiring $$gNg^{-1}=N \text{ for all} g\in G.$$
We write $N\trianglelefteq G$ to mean that $N$ is a normal subgroup.
\end{define}

We gave some concrete examples of normal subgroups, but here is a general
fact: normal subgroups arise naturally as kernels of group homomorphisms.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kernel-normal}
	If $\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism, then $\ker(\varphi)\trianglelefteq G$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	We already know that $\ker(\varphi)$ is a subgroup. If $a\in\ker(\varphi)$
	and $g\in G$, then
	\[
	\varphi(gag^{-1})
	=\varphi(g)\varphi(a)\varphi(g)^{-1}
	=\varphi(g)e_H\varphi(g)^{-1}=e_H.
	\]
	Thus $gag^{-1}\in\ker(\varphi)$, as required.
\end{proof}

We will see later in the course that the converse also holds: every normal
subgroup of $G$ is the kernel of some homomorphism with domain $G$. 

\begin{example}
Recall our example from last time about the group
\[
  S_3=\{1,x,x^2,y,xy,x^2y\},\qquad
  x^3=1,\quad y^2=1,\quad yx=x^2y.
\]

Its subgroups and their inclusions are depicted as follows:

We argued last time that $N\trianglelefteq S_3$ (there we called it $H$)
but that $L$ is not normal. The same kind of computation shows that
$\langle xy\rangle$ and $\langle x^2y\rangle$ are not normal either
(exercise). Thus, by Proposition~\ref{prop:kernel-normal}, none of the three
subgroups of order two can be the kernel of a homomorphism. But is $N$ a
kernel? We will see shortly that it is: $N$ is the kernel of the sign
homomorphism.
\end{example}


\section{More examples: determinant and sign}

\begin{example}[The determinant homomorphism]
Multiplicativity of the determinant says that
\begin{equation}
  \det:\GL_n(\R)\longrightarrow\R^*,
  \qquad A\longmapsto\det(A),
\end{equation}
is a homomorphism, where $\R^*=\R\setminus\{0\}$ is a group under
multiplication. Its kernel is
\[
  \ker(\det)=\SL_n(\R)
  =\{A\in\GL_n(\R):\det(A)=1\}.
\]
Since kernels are normal, $\SL_n(\R)\trianglelefteq\GL_n(\R)$.
\end{example}

Next we construct the sign homomorphism on $S_n$. We give two
constructions: Approach A is combinatorial and builds on Lecture~5, while
Approach B uses linear algebra and the determinant. Throughout, $\{\pm1\}$
denotes the group with two elements under usual multiplication, with
identity $1$. Up to isomorphism, it is the unique group with two elements.

\subsection*{Approach A: parity of permutations}

Recall from Lecture~5 that $\sigma\in S_n$ is \emph{even} if it can be
written as a product of an even number of simple transpositions, and
\emph{odd} otherwise. We proved there that this is well defined: no
permutation can be written both as a product of an even number and as a
product of an odd number of simple transpositions. Since
$\sigma=s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_k}$ with $k=\ell(\sigma)$, the permutation
$\sigma$ is even exactly when $\ell(\sigma)$ is even.

\begin{define}[Sign]
The \emph{sign} of $\sigma\in S_n$ is
\begin{equation}
  \sgn(\sigma)=(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)}=
  \begin{cases}
    +1,&\text{if $\sigma$ is even},\\
    -1,&\text{if $\sigma$ is odd}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{define}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sgn-hom}
The map $\sgn:S_n\to\{\pm1\}$ is a homomorphism.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\sigma,\tau\in S_n$, and write $\sigma$ as a product of $a$ simple
transpositions and $\tau$ as a product of $b$ simple transpositions.
Concatenating the two expressions writes $\sigma\tau$ as a product of $a+b$
simple transpositions. Since parity is well defined,
$(-1)^a=\sgn(\sigma)$ and $(-1)^b=\sgn(\tau)$. Therefore,
\[
  \sgn(\sigma\tau)=(-1)^{a+b}=(-1)^a(-1)^b=\sgn(\sigma)\sgn(\tau).
\]
\end{proof}

In other words, the multiplication rule in $\{\pm1\}$ has exactly the
parity table of permutations:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &\text{even}&\text{odd}\\ \hline
\text{even}&\text{even}&\text{odd}\\
\text{odd}&\text{odd}&\text{even}
\end{array}
\]


\subsection*{Approach B: permutation matrices}

We next construct the sign homomorphism geometrically. For
$\sigma\in S_n$, let $e_1,\dots,e_n$ be the standard basis of $\R^n$ and
define the permutation matrix $[\sigma]$ by
\begin{equation}
  [\sigma]e_i=e_{\sigma(i)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the $i$th column of $[\sigma]$ is $e_{\sigma(i)}$.
If $E_{ij}$ denotes the matrix with a $1$ in row $i$, column $j$, and zeros
elsewhere, then
\begin{equation}
  [\sigma]=\sum_{i=1}^n E_{\sigma(i),i}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}
For $\sigma=(1\ 2\ 3)\in S_3$,
\[
  [\sigma]=
  \begin{pmatrix}
		    0&0&1\\
		    1&0&0\\
		    0&1&0
		  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  [\sigma]
  \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}x_3\\x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\end{example}

\begin{warningbox}
The entry in output position $j$ is $x_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}$.
Indeed, the $i$th basis vector is sent to $e_{\sigma(i)}$, so the entry
originally in position $i$ appears in position $\sigma(i)$.
In column-vector notation,
\[
  [\sigma]
  \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
	    x_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\\x_{\sigma^{-1}(2)}\\\vdots\\x_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}
	  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{warningbox}

\begin{proposition}
The assignment $\sigma\mapsto[\sigma]$ is a homomorphism
$S_n\to\GL_n(\R)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Using right-to-left composition, for every standard basis vector $e_i$ we have
\[
  [\sigma][\tau]e_i
  =[\sigma]e_{\tau(i)}
  =e_{\sigma(\tau(i))}
  =[\sigma\tau]e_i.
\]
Since the matrices agree on every basis vector,
$[\sigma][\tau]=[\sigma\tau]$. Also, $[\id]=I_n$, so
\[
  [\sigma][\sigma^{-1}]=I_n=[\sigma^{-1}][\sigma].
\]
Thus $[\sigma]^{-1}=[\sigma^{-1}]$, which shows that $[\sigma]$ belongs
to $\GL_n(\R)$ and completes the proof.
\end{proof}

Both $[\sigma]$ and $[\sigma^{-1}]$ have integer entries, so their
determinants are integers. Since they are inverse matrices,
\[
  \det([\sigma])\det([\sigma^{-1}])=1.
\]
Hence $\det([\sigma])\in\{+1,-1\}$.

The map $\sigma\mapsto\det([\sigma])$ is the composition of the two
homomorphisms $[\cdot]:S_n\to\GL_n(\R)$ and $\det:\GL_n(\R)\to\R^*$. It is
therefore a homomorphism, since
\[
  \det([\sigma\tau])
  =\det([\sigma][\tau])
  =\det([\sigma])\det([\tau]).
\]
This calculation is an instance of the following general fact, which is
left as an exercise.

\begin{lemma}[Composition of homomorphisms]
Let $G_1,G_2,G_3$ be groups, and let $\varphi:G_1\to G_2$ and
$\psi:G_2\to G_3$ be homomorphisms. Then
$\psi\circ\varphi:G_1\to G_3$ is a homomorphism.
\end{lemma}

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sgn-det}
For every $\sigma\in S_n$, we have $\sgn(\sigma)=\det([\sigma])$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The matrix of a simple transposition exchanges two columns of $I_n$, so it
has determinant $-1$. If $\sigma=s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_k}$ is \emph{any}
expression as a product of simple transpositions, multiplicativity gives
\[
  \det([\sigma])=\prod_{j=1}^k\det([s_{i_j}])=(-1)^k.
\]
Taking a shortest expression, $k=\ell(\sigma)$, gives
$\det([\sigma])=(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)}=\sgn(\sigma)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Approach B gives a second proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:sgn-hom}, and in
fact more. Since the argument above works for \emph{every} expression of
$\sigma$, the number $(-1)^k=\det([\sigma])$ depends only on $\sigma$.
So Approach B independently proves the result of Lecture~5 that parity is
well defined, without counting inversions.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The kernel of sign}

Recall from Lecture~5 the alternating group
\[
  A_n=\{\sigma\in S_n:\sigma\text{ is even}\}.
\]
We can now see it in a new light: $A_n=\ker(\sgn)$. Therefore, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:kernel-normal}, $A_n\trianglelefteq S_n$.

\begin{example}[Sign on $S_3$]
With $x=(1\ 2\ 3)$ and $y=(1\ 3)$, the values of $\sgn$ on
the elements of $S_3$ are:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
  \text{element}&\text{cycle decomposition}&\text{parity}&\sgn \\ \hline
  1    &(1)       &\text{even}&+1\\
  x    &(1\ 2\ 3)&\text{even}&+1\\
  x^2  &(1\ 3\ 2)&\text{even}&+1\\
  y    &(1\ 3)   &\text{odd} &-1\\
  xy   &(2\ 3)   &\text{odd} &-1\\
  x^2y &(1\ 2)   &\text{odd} &-1
\end{array}
\]
Hence
\[
  \ker(\sgn)=A_3=\{1,x,x^2\}=N,
\]
which answers the question from Section~1: $N$ is a kernel. This supplies
another proof that $N\trianglelefteq S_3$.
\end{example}

\section{Simple groups}

There is a very important idea in group theory: start with certain
``simple'' groups and build more complicated groups out of them.

\begin{define}[Simple group]
	Recall from last time that a nontrivial group $G$ is \emph{simple} if
	its only normal subgroups are $\{e_G\}$ and $G$.
\end{define}

As a product of a vast collaborative
effort spanning much of the twentieth century,  mathematicians were able to classify all finite
simple groups completely-- every
finite simple group is isomorphic to one of the following:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Cyclic groups} of prime order, $\Z/p\Z$. These are the only abelian ones.
	\item \textbf{Alternating groups} $A_n$ for $n \geq 5$.
	\item \textbf{Groups of Lie type}, which come in 16 infinite families.
	\item \textbf{The 26 sporadic groups}, which fit into no infinite family; the largest of them is called the Monster.
\end{enumerate}

Later in this course we will show that cyclic groups of prime order and
the alternating groups $A_n$ for $n\geq5$ are simple. How are more
complicated groups built from simple ones? The key tool is the
\emph{quotient group} $G/N$ of a group by a normal subgroup. To construct
it, we first need the notion of a coset.
