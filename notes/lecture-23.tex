\section{Review: conjugation, the center, and the class equation}

In Lecture 22 we considered the action of a group $G$ on itself by
\emph{conjugation}:
\[
  g\cdot x=gxg^{-1}.
\]
This is an action because $1\cdot x=x$ and
$(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$. The orbit of $x$ is its
\emph{conjugacy class}, which we write as
\[
  \Cl_G(x)=\mathcal O_G(x)=\{gxg^{-1}:g\in G\}.
\]
Its stabilizer is the \emph{centralizer}
\[
  C_G(x)=\{g\in G:gxg^{-1}=x\}
        =\{g\in G:gx=xg\}.
\]
For finite $G$, orbit--stabilizer gives
\[
  |\Cl_G(x)|=[G:C_G(x)].
\]
In particular, every conjugacy class size divides $|G|$.
Since the conjugacy classes partition $G$, choosing one representative
$x_i$ from each class gives the \emph{class equation}
\[
  |G|=\sum_i|\Cl_G(x_i)|=\sum_i[G:C_G(x_i)].
\]

Recall also that the \emph{center} is
\[
  Z(G)=\{z\in G:zg=gz\text{ for every }g\in G\}
      =\bigcap_{x\in G}C_G(x).
\]
For any $x\in G$,
\[
  x\in Z(G)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_G(x)=G
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Cl_G(x)=\{x\}.
\]
Thus the central elements are exactly the elements whose conjugacy
classes have size $1$. In the class equation with one summand for
each conjugacy class, \emph{the number of $1$s is $|Z(G)|$}.
There is always at least one, coming from the identity, but other
central elements each contribute another $1$.
Collecting these singleton classes gives
\[
  |G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{x_i\notin Z(G)}[G:C_G(x_i)],
\]
where the sum contains one representative from each noncentral class.
For example, $G$ is abelian exactly when every class has size $1$;
in that case $Z(G)=G$ and the class equation has $|G|$ copies of $1$.

Conjugacy classes also help us find normal subgroups. The center is
normal, since $gzg^{-1}=z$ for every $z\in Z(G)$ and $g\in G$.
More generally, we have the following criterion.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:normal-union}
A subgroup $N\leq G$ is normal if and only if it is a union of
conjugacy classes of $G$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Normality says that $gng^{-1}\in N$ for all $n\in N$ and $g\in G$.
Equivalently, $\Cl_G(n)\subseteq N$ for every $n\in N$.
\end{proof}

For finite $G$, a normal subgroup's order is therefore a sum of
class sizes that includes the identity class. It must also divide
$|G|$ by Lagrange's theorem. These two requirements can be restrictive.

\begin{example}[Normal subgroups of $S_3$]
The class equation is $6=1+2+3$. There is exactly one $1$, so
$|Z(S_3)|=1$ and hence $Z(S_3)=\{1\}$.
A normal subgroup must include
the identity, so its possible orders from unions of classes are
$1,3,4,6$. Of these, only $1,3,6$ divide $6$.
Thus the only nontrivial proper normal subgroup is
\[
  A_3=\{1,(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2)\}.
\]
\end{example}

\section{Cycle decompositions and cycle types}

Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles.  Recall also the following fact

\begin{theorem}[Conjugacy in $S_n$]\label{thm:cycle-type}
Two permutations in $S_n$ are conjugate if and only if they have
the same cycle type, including fixed points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Conjugation relabels a cycle:
\[
  g(i_1\,\cdots\,i_r)g^{-1}=(g(i_1)\,\cdots\,g(i_r)).
\]
It therefore preserves the lengths of disjoint cycles.
Conversely, for two permutations of the same cycle type, match
their cycles of equal lengths and define $g$ by matching the
entries in those cycles in order. The displayed formula then
shows that $g$ conjugates one permutation to the other.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\begin{example}[The class equation of $S_4$]
The possible cycle types and their sizes are as follows.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccl}
  \textbf{Representative} & \textbf{Class size} & \textbf{Count}\\ \hline
  $1$ & $1$ & Identity\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2)$ & $6$ & $\binom42$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3)$ & $8$ & $\binom43\cdot2$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3\,4)$ & $6$ & $3!$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ & $3$ & Three pairings of four entries
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For instance, after choosing the entries of a $3$-cycle there are
two cyclic orders. For a $4$-cycle, putting $1$ first leaves $3!$
orders for the other entries. The class equation is
\[
  24=1+6+8+6+3.
\]
\end{example}
