\section{The class equation of D\texorpdfstring{$_{12}$}{12}}

Recall the regular hexagon with vertices $v_1,\ldots,v_6$ labeled
counterclockwise. Its symmetry group is $D_{12}$: it consists of
six rotations and six reflections, so $|D_{12}|=12$.
Let $x$ be counterclockwise rotation through $60^\circ$, and let
$r$ be reflection across the long diagonal $\overline{v_1v_4}$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  \foreach \i in {1,...,6} {
    \coordinate (v\i) at ({60*(\i-1)}:1.55);
  }
  \draw[thick,gray] (v1)--(v2)--(v3)--(v4)--(v5)--(v6)--cycle;
  \draw[dashed,thick,Plum] (-2.15,0)--(2.15,0);
  \node[below,text=Plum] at (0,0) {axis of $r$};
  \draw[->,thick,courseblue] (15:2.05) arc (15:65:2.05);
  \node[right,text=courseblue] at (35:2.15) {$x$};
  \foreach \i in {1,...,6} {\fill (v\i) circle (2pt);}
  \node[above right] at (v1) {$v_1$};
  \node[above left] at (v2) {$v_2$};
  \node[above left] at (v3) {$v_3$};
  \node[above left] at (v4) {$v_4$};
  \node[below left] at (v5) {$v_5$};
  \node[below right] at (v6) {$v_6$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Thus $x$ sends $v_i$ to $v_{i+1}$ (with subscripts read modulo $6$),
while $r$ fixes $v_1,v_4$ and interchanges $v_2$ with $v_6$ and
$v_3$ with $v_5$. These symmetries generate $D_{12}$ and satisfy
\[
  x^6=r^2=1,\qquad rxr^{-1}=x^{-1}.
\]
The six rotations are $x^k$ and the six reflections are $x^kr$,
for $0\leq k<6$.

Before computing the classes, let us narrow down the possible class
equations. Each class size divides $12$, and each central element
contributes a $1$. 

Note that we have the relation \be{} r x^k r^{-1} = x^{-k} \ee which follows immediatley from the relation $rxr^{-1} = x^{-1}$. 
Hence, a rotation $x^k$ commutes with $r$ exactly when
$x^k=x^{-k}$, or $2k\equiv0\pmod6$. 

No reflection, i.e. the elements $x^kr$, commutes with
$x$, since conjugation by any reflection sends $x$ to $x^{-1}\neq x$.
Thus
\[
  Z(D_{12})=\{1,x^3\},
\]
so there are exactly two $1$s. Moreover, $Z(D_{12})\leq C_{D_{12}}(y)$
for every $y$, so
\[
  [D_{12}:C_{D_{12}}(y)]\text{ divides }[D_{12}:Z(D_{12})]=6.
\]
The remaining class sizes must therefore be $2$, $3$, or $6$,
and must sum to $10$. Writing $10=2a+3b+6c$ with $a,b,c\geq0$,
we see that $b$ is even and $c\leq1$. This leaves just three
possibilities, up to reordering the summands:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  12&=1+1+2+2+2+2+2,\\
  12&=1+1+2+2+3+3,\\
  12&=1+1+2+2+6.
\end{aligned}
\]
We now compute the classes to decide which possibility occurs.

Since $x$ and $r$ generate $D_{12}$, we find a conjugacy class by starting with an element and
repeatedly conjugating by $x$ and $r$.

For a rotation $x^k$, conjugation by $x$ does nothing, while
conjugation by $r$ inverts it:
\[
  x x^k x^{-1}=x^k,\qquad
  r x^k r^{-1}=(rxr^{-1})^k=x^{-k}.
\]
Further conjugations can only interchange $x^k$ and $x^{-k}$.
Both occur, so $\Cl_{D_{12}}(x^k)=\{x^k,x^{-k}\}$, with a
singleton when $x^k=x^{-k}$. Hence the conjugacy classes of the
rotation elements are
\[
  \{1\},\qquad \{x^3\},\qquad
  \{x,x^5\},\qquad \{x^2,x^4\}.
\]
For the reflections, conjugating $r$ by $x$ gives
$xrx^{-1}=x^2r$, using $rx^{-1}=xr$. More generally, the relations give
\[
  x^a(x^kr)x^{-a}=x^{k+2a}r,\qquad
  r(x^kr)r^{-1}=x^{-k}r.
\]
These conjugations preserve the parity of $k$, and conjugation by
powers of $x$ reaches all three exponents of the same parity.
Hence the two reflection classes are
\[
  \{r,x^2r,x^4r\},\qquad \{xr,x^3r,x^5r\}.
\]
The class equation is therefore
\[
  12=1+1+2+2+3+3.
\]
The corresponding disjoint union of conjugacy classes is
\[
  D_{12}
  =\underbrace{\{1\}}_{1}
  \sqcup\underbrace{\{x^3\}}_{1}
  \sqcup\underbrace{\{x,x^5\}}_{2}
  \sqcup\underbrace{\{x^2,x^4\}}_{2}
  \sqcup\underbrace{\{r,x^2r,x^4r\}}_{3}
  \sqcup\underbrace{\{xr,x^3r,x^5r\}}_{3}.
\]
This is the second of our three candidates, with the two $1$s
corresponding to $1$ and $x^3$ in the center.

Now let's try to understand the normal subgroups of $D_{12}$

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:normal-union}
A subgroup $N\leq G$ is normal if and only if it is a union of
conjugacy classes of $G$.
\end{proposition}


For finite $G$, a normal subgroup's order is therefore a sum of
class sizes that includes the identity class. It must also divide
$|G|$ by Lagrange's theorem.  In $D_{12}$, a normal subgroup must therefore contain $\{1\}$
and some of the other five classes in
\[
  12=1+1+2+2+3+3.
\]
Its order must divide $12$, so the possible orders are
$1,2,3,4,6,12$. That doesn't seeem very helpful! 

However, a union of classes with an allowable
size need not be a subgroup: we must also check closure.
For example, $\{1\}\sqcup\{r,x^2r,x^4r\}$ has size $4$, but
\[
  (x^2r)r=x^2
\]
is not in this union.

We can find all the normal subgroups by separating two cases.

\textbf{Only rotations.}
If $N\subseteq\langle x\rangle$, then $N$ is one of the subgroups
of this cyclic group of order $6$:
\[
  \{1\},\qquad \langle x^3\rangle,\qquad
  \langle x^2\rangle,\qquad \langle x\rangle.
\]
Each is normal in $D_{12}$: conjugation by $x$ fixes rotations,
and conjugation by $r$ inverts them. Their decompositions into
classes appear in the table below.

\textbf{At least one reflection.}
If $x^kr\in N$, normality forces its entire conjugacy class into
$N$, so $x^{k+2}r\in N$ as well. Closure then gives
\[
  (x^{k+2}r)(x^kr)^{-1}=x^2\in N.
\]
Thus $N$ contains $\langle x^2,x^kr\rangle$, which consists of
three rotations and the three reflections in the class of $x^kr$.
This subgroup has order $6$, so Lagrange's theorem forces $N$
to equal it or all of $D_{12}$. For even $k$ it is
$\langle x^2,r\rangle$; for odd $k$ it is $\langle x^2,xr\rangle$.
Both have index $2$ and hence are normal.

\Needspace{16\baselineskip}
The complete list is therefore:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \textbf{Normal subgroup} & \textbf{Union of conjugacy classes} & \textbf{Order}\\ \hline
  $\{1\}$ & $\{1\}$ & $1$\\[4pt]
  $\langle x^3\rangle$ & $\{1\}\sqcup\{x^3\}$ & $1+1=2$\\[4pt]
  $\langle x^2\rangle$ & $\{1\}\sqcup\{x^2,x^4\}$ & $1+2=3$\\[4pt]
  $\langle x\rangle$ & $\{1\}\sqcup\{x^3\}\sqcup\{x,x^5\}\sqcup\{x^2,x^4\}$ & $1+1+2+2=6$\\[4pt]
  $\langle x^2,r\rangle$ & $\{1\}\sqcup\{x^2,x^4\}\sqcup\{r,x^2r,x^4r\}$ & $1+2+3=6$\\[4pt]
  $\langle x^2,xr\rangle$ & $\{1\}\sqcup\{x^2,x^4\}\sqcup\{xr,x^3r,x^5r\}$ & $1+2+3=6$\\[4pt]
  $D_{12}$ & All six classes & $1+1+2+2+3+3=12$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In particular, there is no normal subgroup of order $4$.
The class equation supplies the possible pieces; the subgroup
condition determines which unions actually occur.

\section{Conjugacy classes and the class equation of S\texorpdfstring{$_5$}{5}}

Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles.  Recall also the following fact

\begin{theorem}[Conjugacy in $S_n$]\label{thm:cycle-type}
Two permutations in $S_n$ are conjugate if and only if they have
the same cycle type, including fixed points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Conjugation relabels a cycle:
\[
  g(i_1\,\cdots\,i_r)g^{-1}=(g(i_1)\,\cdots\,g(i_r)).
\]
It therefore preserves the lengths of disjoint cycles.
Conversely, for two permutations of the same cycle type, match
their cycles of equal lengths and define $g$ by matching the
entries in those cycles in order. The displayed formula then
shows that $g$ conjugates one permutation to the other.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The class equation of $S_5$]\label{ex:s5}
There are seven cycle types in $S_5$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccl}
  \textbf{Representative} & \textbf{Class size} & \textbf{Parity} & \textbf{Count}\\ \hline
  $1$ & $1$ & Even & Identity\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2)$ & $10$ & Odd & $\binom52$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3)$ & $20$ & Even & $\binom53\cdot2$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3\,4)$ & $30$ & Odd & $\binom54\cdot3!$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)$ & $24$ & Even & $4!$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ & $15$ & Even & $\binom54\cdot3$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3)(4\,5)$ & $20$ & Odd & $\binom53\cdot2$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Thus
\[
  120=1+10+20+30+24+15+20.
\]
The even permutations have total size $1+20+24+15=60$.
This is not yet the class equation of $A_5$: conjugation in $A_5$
allows only even conjugating permutations.
\end{example}

\section{Conjugacy in S\texorpdfstring{$_5$}{5} versus A\texorpdfstring{$_5$}{5}}

To compare conjugacy in $S_5$ and $A_5$, we use the following
general criterion for $S_n$ and $A_n$.

For $\sigma\in A_n$, we have
\[
  \Cl_{A_n}(\sigma)\subseteq\Cl_{S_n}(\sigma),\qquad
  C_{A_n}(\sigma)=C_{S_n}(\sigma)\cap A_n.
\]
The first inclusion can be strict: a class in $S_n$ may split when
we restrict conjugation to $A_n$.

\begin{lemma}[Splitting of conjugacy classes]\label{lem:splitting}
Let $n\geq2$ and $\sigma\in A_n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}]
  \item If $C_{S_n}(\sigma)\subseteq A_n$, then
  $\Cl_{S_n}(\sigma)$ splits into two $A_n$-classes of equal size, and
  \[
    |\Cl_{A_n}(\sigma)|=\tfrac12|\Cl_{S_n}(\sigma)|.
  \]
  \item If $C_{S_n}(\sigma)$ contains an odd permutation, then
  $\Cl_{A_n}(\sigma)=\Cl_{S_n}(\sigma)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $H=C_{S_n}(\sigma)$ and $K=H\cap A_n=C_{A_n}(\sigma)$.
Orbit--stabilizer gives
\[
  |\Cl_{S_n}(\sigma)|=\frac{n!}{|H|},\qquad
  |\Cl_{A_n}(\sigma)|=\frac{n!/2}{|K|}.
\]
If $H\subseteq A_n$, then $K=H$, giving the factor $1/2$.
Fix an odd permutation $t$. Every conjugator in $S_n$ is either
even or belongs to the coset $A_nt$, so the $S_n$-class is the union
of the $A_n$-classes of $\sigma$ and $t\sigma t^{-1}$.
Conjugation by $t$ gives a bijection between these two classes,
so they have equal size. The factor $1/2$ shows that they are distinct.

If $H$ contains an odd permutation, the sign map on $H$ is
surjective with kernel $K$. Hence $|H|=2|K|$, and the two class
sizes are equal. The inclusion then gives equality of the classes.
Equivalently, an odd conjugator can be multiplied by an odd
element of $H$ to obtain an even conjugator with the same effect
on $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Two $5$-cycles that are not conjugate in $A_5$]
Let $c=(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)$ and $d=(1\,2\,3\,5\,4)$.
They are conjugate in $S_5$ because $d=(4\,5)c(4\,5)^{-1}$.
The centralizer of $c$ in $S_5$ is
\[
  C_{S_5}(c)=\langle c\rangle.
\]
Indeed, a permutation commuting with $c$ is determined by its
value at $1$, since $h(c^j(1))=c^j(h(1))$. There are at most five
choices, and the five powers of $c$ all commute with $c$.
These powers are even.

If an even $h$ also satisfied $hch^{-1}=d$, then
$(4\,5)^{-1}h$ would belong to $C_{S_5}(c)$, which is impossible
because it is odd. Thus $c$ and $d$ lie in distinct $A_5$-classes.
Each class has size $60/5=12$.
\end{example}

\section{The class equation and simplicity of A\texorpdfstring{$_5$}{5}}

The even cycle types in $S_5$ are the identity, $3$-cycles,
double transpositions, and $5$-cycles.
The centralizer of $(1\,2\,3)$ contains the odd permutation
$(4\,5)$, so its class does not split. Likewise, $(1\,2)$ is an
odd element centralizing $(1\,2)(3\,4)$, so that class does not
split. The $5$-cycles split into two classes as above.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{example}[The class equation of $A_5$]
The classes have the following representatives and sizes.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
  \textbf{Representative} & \textbf{Class size} & \textbf{Centralizer order in $A_5$}\\ \hline
  $1$ & $1$ & $60$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3)$ & $20$ & $3$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ & $15$ & $4$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)$ & $12$ & $5$\\[2pt]
  $(1\,2\,3\,5\,4)$ & $12$ & $5$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Hence
\[
  60=1+20+15+12+12.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{define}[Simple group]
A nontrivial group is \emph{simple} if its only normal subgroups
are $\{1\}$ and the whole group.
\end{define}

\begin{theorem}
The group $A_5$ is simple.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $N\trianglelefteq A_5$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:normal-union},
its order is $1$ plus a sum of some of $20,15,12,12$.
If $N\neq\{1\}$, then $|N|\geq13$. If it is also proper, the
only divisors of $60$ this large are $15,20,30$.
None of these is $1$ plus a sum of some of $20,15,12,12$.
Thus there is no nontrivial proper normal subgroup.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
More generally, $A_n$ is simple for every $n\geq5$.
The argument above proves the case $n=5$.
\end{remark}
