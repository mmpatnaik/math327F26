\section{Review and examples}

Recall that a group is a triple $(G,\cdot,e)$ consisting of a set $G$, a
composition law $\cdot$, and an identity element $e$. Depending on the
context, the identity may also be denoted by $1$, $e_G$, or $1_G$.

Our first examples were the additive groups
\be
(\Z,+,0)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
(\Z/n\Z,+,\overline{0}),
\ee
the general linear group
\be
\GL_n(\R)=\{A\in M_n(\R):\det(A)\ne0\},
\ee
and the permutation group $S_n$. The group $\GL_n(\R)$ carries additional
geometric structure: it is a topological space, a differentiable manifold,
and a real algebraic group. In particular, it is a \emph{Lie group}.

\section{Permutations and cycle notation}

Let
\be
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.
\ee
A permutation of $[n]$ is a bijection $p\colon[n]\to[n]$. It may be displayed
in two-line notation as
\be
p=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&\cdots&n\\
p(1)&p(2)&\cdots&p(n)
\end{pmatrix}.
\ee
Cycle notation is often more economical. For example, the cycle
\be
p=(1\ 2\ 3)
\ee
sends $1$ to $2$, $2$ to $3$, and $3$ to $1$, while fixing every other
element. Thus, as a permutation of $[5]$,
\be
(1\ 2\ 3)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3&4&5\\
2&3&1&4&5
\end{pmatrix}.
\ee

If $p$ and $q$ are permutations, we use the convention
\be
qp=q\circ p;
\ee
thus the rightmost permutation is applied first.

\begin{example}[Composing disjoint cycles]
Let $q=(2\ 3\ 4)$ and $p=(1\ 5)$. Then
\be
qp=(2\ 3\ 4)(1\ 5)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3&4&5\\
5&3&4&2&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\ee
The cycles are disjoint, so they commute:
\be
(2\ 3\ 4)(1\ 5)=(1\ 5)(2\ 3\ 4).
\ee
\end{example}

The empty cycle notation, or equivalently $(1)$, denotes the identity
permutation. A cycle of the form $(a\ b)$ is called a \emph{transposition}.

\begin{remark}
Every permutation is a product of transpositions. Indeed, a cycle can be
written as
\be
(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)
=(a_1\ a_k)(a_1\ a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\ a_2).
\ee
\end{remark}

\section{The symmetric group}

Let $S_n$ denote the set of all permutations of $[n]$.

\begin{proposition}
The triple $(S_n,\circ,\id)$ is a group.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The composite of two bijections is again a bijection, so $S_n$ is closed
under composition. Composition of functions is associative, the identity
map $\id$ is the identity element, and the inverse of a bijection is again a
bijection. Hence all the group axioms hold.
\end{proof}

For example, the permutation
\be
p=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3&4&5\\
2&3&1&5&4
\end{pmatrix}
\ee
has the cycle decomposition
\be
p=(1\ 2\ 3)(4\ 5).
\ee
Disjoint cycles may be written in any order, and cycles of length one are
usually omitted. More generally, a permutation can have several expressions
as a product of cycles. For instance,
\be
(1\ 2\ 3)=(1\ 2)(2\ 3)=(1\ 3)(1\ 2).
\ee

\subsection{The group $S_3$}

The six elements of $S_3$ are the identity, the three transpositions, and the
two $3$-cycles:
\be
S_3=\{\id,(1\ 2),(1\ 3),(2\ 3),(1\ 2\ 3),(1\ 3\ 2)\}.
\ee
Set
\be
x=(1\ 2\ 3)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
y=(1\ 3).
\ee
Then
\be
x^2=(1\ 3\ 2),
  \qquad x^3=\id,
  \qquad y^2=\id,
\ee
and direct calculation gives
\be
yx=x^2y.
\ee
Consequently,
\be
S_3=\{\id,x,x^2,y,xy,yx\}.
\ee
In particular, $S_3$ is not abelian: for example, $xy\ne yx$.

\begin{example}[A permutation of eight letters]
Consider
\be
\sigma=(1\ 5\ 7)(2\ 6)(3\ 4)\in S_8.
\ee
The cycles are disjoint, and therefore
\be
\sigma=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\
5&6&4&3&7&2&1&8
\end{pmatrix}.
\ee
Thus $1\mapsto5$, $5\mapsto7$, $7\mapsto1$; the elements $2$ and $6$ are
interchanged, as are $3$ and $4$; and $8$ is fixed.
\end{example}
