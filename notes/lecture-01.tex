\section{Axioms for a group}

To define a group, we begin with a set $G$ equipped with a \emph{composition law}
\be
\cdot \colon G\times G\longrightarrow G,
  \qquad (g,h)\longmapsto g\cdot h,
\ee
and a distinguished element $e\in G$, called the \emph{identity}. Thus the
basic data are written $(G,\cdot,e)$. We often suppress the dot and write
$gh$ in place of $g\cdot h$.

\begin{definition}[Group]
The data $(G,\cdot,e)$ form a \emph{group} if the following three conditions
hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}]
  \item \textbf{Associativity.} For all $g,h,k\in G$,
  \be
  g\cdot(h\cdot k)=(g\cdot h)\cdot k.
  \ee
  \item \textbf{Identity.} For every $g\in G$,
  \be
  e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g.
  \ee
  \item \textbf{Inverses.} For every $g\in G$, there exists $h\in G$ such
  that
  \be
  h\cdot g=e
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    g\cdot h=e.
  \ee
    The first equality says that $h$ is a \textit{left inverse} of $g$; the second says
  that it is a \textit{right inverse.} So inverse really means `double sided' inverse.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}


Associativity does \emph{not} say that
\be
g\cdot h=h\cdot g.
\ee
An operation satisfying this latter identity for all elements is called \emph{commutative}.


\begin{definition}[Abelian group]
	A group whose operation is commutative is called \emph{abelian}.
\end{definition}

It is also useful to observe the following simple result.


\begin{lemma}[Uniqueness of inverses]
	The element $h$ in axiom \textbf{(3)} is unique.

	\begin{proof}
		Suppose $h_1$ and $h_2$ both satisfy axiom \textbf{(3)} for $g$. Then
		\be
		h_1g=h_2g=e.
		\ee
				Multiplying on the right by $h_2$ and using associativity gives
		\be
		(h_1g)h_2=(h_2g)h_2,
		\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
		h_1(gh_2)=h_2(gh_2).
		\ee
				Since $gh_2=e$, it follows that $h_1=h_2$. We write the unique inverse of
		$g$ as $g^{-1}$.
	\end{proof}
\end{lemma}


\section{Associativity, cyclic groups, and order}


The associativity axiom allows us to unambiguously refer to \be{} g \cdot (h \cdot k) \mbox{ and } (g \cdot h) \cdot k \mbox{ as } \ee \be{} g \cdot h \cdot k. \ee An easy induction then allows us to define, given $g_1, \ldots, g_n \in G$ an element
\be{}
g_1g_2\cdots g_n
\ee
by removing parantheses in any order. Note again that we cannot change the order of the factors in the product (unless the group is abelian).


For $x\in G$ and a positive
integer $n$, we also write
\be
x^n=\underbrace{x\cdot x\cdots x}_{n\text{ factors}}.
.\ee If $n=0$ we just set \be{} x^0 =e \ee where $e$ is the identity of the group.Also if $n >0$ and $x^{-1}$ refers to the (unique) inverse of $x$, we set \be{} \label{xn:inv} x^{-n} = \underbrace{ x^{-1} \cdot x^{-1} \cdots x^{-1} }_{n\text{ factors}}. \ee One can immediately verify then that \be{} x^{-n} = (x^n)^{-1}, \text{ i.e. } (x^n)(x^{-n}) = \underbrace{x\cdot x\cdots x}_{n\text{ factors}}  \underbrace{ x^{-1} \cdot x^{-1} \cdots x^{-1} }_{n\text{ factors}} = e  \ee 

\newcommand{\ord}{\mathrm{ord}}

\begin{definition} If $x \neq e \in G$, we define its order and write $\ord_G(x) = \ord(x) = n$ if $n$ is the smallest positive integer such that $x^n=e$. If no such $n$ exists, we write $\ord(x)=\infty$ and say $x$ has infinite order. If $x=e$, we often say that it has order $0$ (and is the unique such element with this property) \end{definition}
	
\begin{remark} Confusingly, we also say that if $G$ is finite as a set then $G$ has finite order. If $G$ is infinite, we write $|G|=\infty$ and say that
$G$ has infinite order. \end{remark} 


Here is a simple result.

\begin{lemma} Let $(G, \cdot, e)$ be a group and $x \in G$. Write \be{} \langle x \rangle:= \{ x^n \mid n \in \Z \}. \ee Then $\langle x \rangle$ is also a group with same composition and identity as $G$. It is called the cyclic (sub) group generated by $x$. 
\begin{proof} What do we need to verify? The main thing is to check that \be{} x^n x^m = x^{n+m}. \ee Think through this on your own, first for $n, m$ both positive, and then in general. From here and \eqref{xn:inv}, the inverse axiom follows. \end{proof} \end{lemma} 

Here is a question to think about: certainly if $G$ has finite order, every element has finite order. But is the converse true? i.e. if every element of $G$ has finite order, then is $G$ a finite group? Before we answer it, let's turn to examples. 

\section{Examples}

\begin{example}[The integers under addition]
The triple
\be
(\Z,+,0)
\ee
 is a group. Its composition law is
\be
\Z\times\Z\longrightarrow\Z,
  \qquad (m,n)\longmapsto m+n.
\ee
The identity element is $0$, and the inverse of $m$ is $-m$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The integers under multiplication: a non-example]
The triple
\be
(\Z,\times,1)
\ee
is \emph{not} a group, although it is a semigroup. Multiplication is
associative and $1$ is an identity, so axioms \textbf{(1)} and \textbf{(2)}
hold. Axiom \textbf{(3)} fails because most integers do not have
multiplicative inverses in $\Z$.
\end{example}


\begin{example}[Integers modulo $n$]
Let $n$ be a positive integer. The set of residue classes modulo $n$ is
\be
\Z/n\Z=\{\overline{0},\overline{1},\overline{2},\ldots,
  \overline{n-1}\}.
\ee
For example,
\begin{align*}
  \overline{0}&=\{0,\pm n,\pm 2n,\pm 3n,\ldots\},\\
  \overline{1}&=\{1,1\pm n,1\pm 2n,1\pm 3n,\ldots\}.
\end{align*}
Every integer can be written $m=an+r$ with $0\le r<n$, and then
$\overline{m}=\overline{r}$. Addition is defined by
\be
\overline{m}+\overline{\ell}=\overline{m+\ell}.
\ee
Thus
\be
(\Z/n\Z,+,\overline{0})
\ee
is a group, called the \emph{cyclic group of order $n$}.
\end{example}

In the preceding examples that are groups, the group operation is both
associative and commutative.


\begin{example}[The general linear group]
The set
\be
\GL_n(\R)=\{A:A\text{ is an invertible }n\times n
  \text{ matrix with entries in }\R\}
\ee
is a group under matrix multiplication. Its identity is the identity matrix
\be
I_n=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 &        & 0\\
      & \ddots &  \\
    0 &        & 1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\ee
Thus the group is written $(\GL_n(\R),\cdot,I_n)$, and the composition law
sends $(A,B)$ to $AB$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The permutation group]
The permutation group is
\be
S_n=\{\text{all permutations of }\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\}.
\ee
Its group operation is composition of permutations.
\end{example}
