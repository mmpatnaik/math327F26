\section{Axioms for a group}

To define a group, we begin with a set $G$ equipped with a \emph{composition law}
\be
\cdot \colon G\times G\longrightarrow G,
  \qquad (g,h)\longmapsto g\cdot h,
\ee
and a distinguished element $e\in G$, called the \emph{identity}. Thus the
basic data are written $(G,\cdot,e)$. We often suppress the dot and write
$gh$ in place of $g\cdot h$.

\begin{definition}[Group]
The data $(G,\cdot,e)$ form a \emph{group} if the following three conditions
hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}]
  \item \textbf{Associativity.} For all $g,h,k\in G$,
  \be
  g\cdot(h\cdot k)=(g\cdot h)\cdot k.
  \ee
  \item \textbf{Identity.} For every $g\in G$,
  \be
  e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g.
  \ee
  \item \textbf{Inverses.} For every $g\in G$, there exists $h\in G$ such
  that
  \be
  h\cdot g=e
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    g\cdot h=e.
  \ee
    The first equality says that $h$ is a \textit{left inverse} of $g$; the second says
  that it is a \textit{right inverse}. Thus an inverse is a two-sided inverse.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}


Associativity does \emph{not} say that
\be
g\cdot h=h\cdot g.
\ee
An operation satisfying this latter identity for all elements is called \emph{commutative}.


\begin{definition}[Abelian group]
	A group whose operation is commutative is called \emph{abelian}.
\end{definition}

It is also useful to observe the following simple result.


\begin{lemma}[Uniqueness of inverses]
	The element $h$ in axiom \textbf{(3)} is unique.

	\begin{proof}
		Suppose $h_1$ and $h_2$ both satisfy axiom \textbf{(3)} for $g$. Then
		\be
		h_1g=h_2g=e.
		\ee
				Multiplying on the right by $h_2$ and using associativity gives
		\be
		(h_1g)h_2=(h_2g)h_2,
		\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
		h_1(gh_2)=h_2(gh_2).
		\ee
				Since $gh_2=e$, it follows that $h_1=h_2$. We write the unique inverse of
		$g$ as $g^{-1}$.
	\end{proof}
\end{lemma}


\section{Associativity, cyclic groups, and order}


The associativity axiom allows us to write both
\be
g\cdot(h\cdot k)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
(g\cdot h)\cdot k
\ee
simply as
\be
g\cdot h\cdot k.
\ee
More generally, given $g_1,\ldots,g_n\in G$, repeated application of
associativity allows us to define the element
\be
g_1g_2\cdots g_n
\ee
independently of how parentheses are inserted. Note again that we cannot
change the order of the factors in the product unless the group is abelian.


For $x\in G$ and a positive
integer $n$, we also write
\be
x^n=\underbrace{x\cdot x\cdots x}_{n\text{ factors}}.
\ee
If $n=0$, we set
\be
x^0=e,
\ee
where $e$ is the identity of the group. If $n>0$ and $x^{-1}$ denotes the
unique inverse of $x$, we set
\be
x^{-n}=\underbrace{x^{-1}\cdot x^{-1}\cdots x^{-1}}_{n\text{ factors}}.
\ee
It then follows that
\be
\label{xn:inv}
x^{-n}=(x^n)^{-1}.
\ee
Indeed,
\be
(x^n)(x^{-n})
=\underbrace{x\cdot x\cdots x}_{n\text{ factors}}
 \underbrace{x^{-1}\cdot x^{-1}\cdots x^{-1}}_{n\text{ factors}}
=e.
\ee

\newcommand{\ord}{\mathrm{ord}}

\begin{definition}[Order of an element]
Let $x\in G$. The \emph{order} of $x$, denoted $\ord_G(x)$ or $\ord(x)$,
is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $x^n=e$. If no such $n$
exists, we write $\ord(x)=\infty$ and say that $x$ has infinite order. In
particular, $\ord(e)=1$.
\end{definition}
	
\begin{remark} Confusingly, we also say that if $G$ is finite as a set then $G$ has finite order. If $G$ is infinite, we write $|G|=\infty$ and say that
$G$ has infinite order. \end{remark} 


Here is a simple result.

\begin{lemma}
Let $(G,\cdot,e)$ be a group and let $x\in G$. Write
\be
\langle x\rangle:=\{x^n\mid n\in\Z\}.
\ee
Then $\langle x\rangle$ is a group. It is called the \emph{cyclic
subgroup generated by $x$}.

\begin{proof}
For all $m,n\in\Z$, associativity and the definitions of positive and
negative powers give
\be
x^n x^m=x^{n+m},
\ee
so $\langle x\rangle$ is closed under the group operation. Associativity is
inherited from $G$, the identity $x^0=e$ belongs to $\langle x\rangle$, and
\eqref{xn:inv} shows that the inverse of $x^n$ is $x^{-n}$. Thus
$\langle x\rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$.
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

Here is a question to think about: certainly, if $G$ has finite order, every
element has finite order. But is the converse true? That is, if every element
of $G$ has finite order, must $G$ be finite? Before we answer this question,
let us turn to examples.
